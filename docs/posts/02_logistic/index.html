Introduction

Post 2

After completing the data collection and cleaning stages of the project, the next step was developing models to estimate in-game win probability. The first models I explored were logistic regression models, inspired by the research paper A Bayesian Logistic Regression Framework for In-Game Win Probability Estimation in NCAA Basketball.

Because I had previously worked with logistic regression in coursework, I began with a frequentist logistic regression model before extending the approach to a Bayesian structure. These models provided a different perspective from the MLE and Dynamic Bayes models explored later in the project. Rather than directly calculating probabilities from historical game data, logistic regression learns a relationship between game-state variables like time and score with the probability of winning.

Frequentist Logistic Regression Models

The objective of the logistic regression models was to predict the probability that the home team would eventually win the game. The response variable, home_win, was binary, making logistic regression a natural modeling choice.

Unlike the MLE and Dynamic Bayes methods, which estimate probabilities by examining historical outcomes at specific score and time combinations, logistic regression fits a mathematical model to the data. The model learns how variables such as lead_differential and time_elapsed influence the likelihood of winning and then converts those relationships into predicted probabilities.

All logistic regression models were trained using the cleaned 2025 season dataset.

Without Team Rating

The first model used only time_elapsed, lead_diff, and their interaction term.

\(home\_win = time\_elapsed + lead\_diff + time\_elapsed*lead\_diff\)

This model assumes that win probability depends entirely on the current game situation. The score differential captures who is leading, while the interaction term allows the importance of a lead to change as the game progresses. For example, a 10-point lead early in the game is much less valuable than a 10-point lead in the final minutes.

To visualize the model, predicted probabilities were generated using the ggpredict() function from the ggeffects package and plotted across a range of score differentials.

The resulting curves show how the model’s estimate of home-team win probability changes throughout a game. As expected, larger leads correspond to higher probabilities, and the effect of the lead becomes increasingly important as time expires.

With Team Rating

The second frequentist model incorporated team strength through the rating_diff variable. The team rating data was sourced from TeamRankings.

\(home\_win = time\_elapsed + lead\_diff + rating\_diff + time\_elapsed*lead\_diff + time\_elapsed*rating\_diff\)

This version extends the previous model by accounting for the relative quality of the two teams. A positive rating_diff indicates that the home team is stronger, while a negative value indicates the away team is stronger.

Including team ratings shifts the predicted probabilities at different lead differentials closer together at the start of the game. In the previous model, a game with a lead differential of 0 begins near a 66% win probability because team strength is not considered, but the model does realize that historically the home team wins more games. Once ratings are included, at the start of a game with a lead differential of 0 the win probability of the home team increases to around 75% before the game even starts.

As the game progresses, the influence of lead differential becomes increasingly important, and team rating becomes less important causing the predictions from both logistic regression models to become more similar late in games.

Bayesian Logistic Regression Model

While the frequentist and Bayesian logistic regression models share the same underlying structure, they differ in how the model parameters are estimated.

Frequentist logistic regression produces a single estimate for each model coefficient. Bayesian logistic regression instead treats the coefficients as random variables and combines prior information with the observed data to estimate a distribution of plausible values for each parameter. This approach naturally provides measures of uncertainty alongside the predicted probabilities.

The Bayesian model was fit using the brms package

\(home\_win = time\_scaled + lead\_scaled + rating\_scaled + time\_scaled*lead\_scaled + time\_scaled*rating\_scaled\)

Initially, the model was difficult to fit because of the size of the dataset. To improve computational efficiency, all predictor variables were scaled before training. Even with these adjustments, the model required substantially more computation than the frequentist models and was ultimately trained using a high-performance computing (HPC) cluster.

Once the model finished training, predicted probabilities and the intervals were generated.

The Bayesian logistic regression model produced results that were different to the frequentist models at the start of games, as a lead differential of zero at the start of a game resulted in a win probability of around 62%. This change in win probability is likely due to adding in the prior beliefs. However, as the game goes on the Bayesian model starts to look a lot more like the frequentist models. This is not surprising because the large amount of training data provides substantial information about the relationships between the predictors and the response variable. As a result, the observed data dominates the prior beliefs, causing the Bayesian and frequentist estimates to converge.

One advantage of the Bayesian approach, however, is that it provides credible intervals that quantify uncertainty in the predicted probabilities. This allows the model to communicate not only the most likely outcome but also the confidence associated with that prediction.

Adjusting for Neutral Site Games

The final modification explored in the logistic regression models was accounting for neutral-site games.

Both logistic regression models implicitly learn a home-court advantage because, historically, home teams win more often than away teams. However, many important college basketball games, including all NCAA Tournament games, are played at neutral sites where neither team receives a true home-court advantage.

In the 2025 season dataset, approximately 11.8% of games were played at neutral locations. To account for this, I used home-court advantage estimates from TeamRankings, which suggest that playing at home is worth approximately 3.3 rating points.

For true home games, 3.3 points were added to the home team’s rating differential. For neutral-site games, no adjustment was applied.

The adjustment shifted the regression curves slightly upward, reflecting the additional advantage awarded to the home team. The results were so minimal because the model already implcitly adjusts for home court advantage, and changing team rating only really affects the start of the game win probability as team rating has less of an effect at the the game goes on.

The same adjustment was then incorporated into the Bayesian logistic regression model.

Conclusion

The logistic regression models provided an important first step in modeling in-game win probability. Unlike the methods examined later in the project, logistic regression learns a smooth mathematical relationship between game state variables and winning rather than relying directly on historical frequencies at specific score and time combinations.

Developing both frequentist and Bayesian versions of the model also provided an opportunity to compare two different statistical approaches. While the models produced similar predictions, the Bayesian approach introduced uncertainty estimates and demonstrated how prior information can be incorporated into a predictive model.

In the next post, I explore a fundamentally different approach to win probability estimation through the MLE and Dynamic Bayes methods. Rather than fitting a regression model, these methods estimate probabilities directly from historical game outcomes and provide an alternative way to model in-game win probability.
